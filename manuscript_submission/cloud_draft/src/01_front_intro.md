%%TITLE Phase-Robust Active Self-Test for Fault Localization in Mixed-Signal Sensor Interfaces: A Simulation Study

%%ABSTRACT
A fault in a sensor signal chain is difficult to localize from the output alone, because the sensor, the analog front-end (AFE) and the analog-to-digital converter (ADC) contribute to a single composite transfer. This simulation study examines a two-point active self-test in which a two-level reference is applied at the AFE input and then directly at the ADC input, while a monitor channel measures the reference levels. Gain and offset estimates from settled plateaus are combined with transient descriptors obtained from a whole-transient fit with a free edge time, so that the descriptors do not depend on where a reference edge falls between two ADC samples. A small multilayer perceptron assigns each record to a faulty layer. Under a protocol frozen before the validation data were generated, the layer macro-F1 was {{ARM_bh_PRR_L}} in a behavioral model and {{ARM_sp_PRR_L}} in an ngspice model, each on its own test grid. It varied by at most {{G_PRR_MAX}} across five edge phases, against {{G_bh_R1_E2_RANGE}} for fixed-sample descriptors, and the behavioral-to-ngspice loss on a matched grid was {{E_DROP}} (95% CI {{CI:E_DROP_LO:E_DROP_HI}}). AFE–ADC cross-confusion did not exceed {{C_MAX}} at reference amplitudes 20% below and above nominal. Healthy records and sensor bias and gain faults were poorly recognized, and no hardware was tested.

# Introduction <<sec:intro>>

When the reading of an embedded sensor node drifts or becomes noisy, the maintenance decision depends on where the cause lies. A degraded sensing element calls for replacement of the probe, whereas a shifted amplifier offset, a narrowed filter bandwidth or a gain error in the converter calls for recalibration or replacement of the electronics. The measured signal passes through all of these stages before it is digitized [@fraden2016], and at the output their static errors combine into one gain and one offset. A change of the composite gain can therefore be produced by the sensor, by the analog front-end (AFE) or by the analog-to-digital converter (ADC), and the output record does not say which.

Sensor validation has dealt with this problem for a long time. The self-validating sensor reports, together with each measurement, an estimate of its on-line uncertainty obtained from internal diagnostics [@henry1993; @feng2007], and a large literature detects and classifies sensor faults such as bias, drift, scaling errors and dropouts from the measured signal or from redundancy among several sensors [@li2020; @balaban2009; @isermann2006]. These methods judge the measurement chain mainly through the signal that it delivers. They are effective at deciding that a channel has become unreliable, and less suited to deciding which stage of the electronics is responsible, because the stages are not excited separately.

Separate excitation requires a known stimulus inside the chain. Integrated converters for sensor signal chains already contain such provisions: a detector that flags a reference voltage that is too low, an internal test voltage that checks the gain stage, and burnout currents that reveal an open sensor connection [@mccarthy2022; @adi_an1320]. A two-point reference test generalizes this idea. A two-level reference replaces the sensor at the AFE input, so that its response passes through the AFE and the ADC, and a second two-level reference is applied directly to the ADC input, so that its response depends on the ADC alone. From the second response the ADC transfer can be estimated and removed, and what remains of the first response describes the AFE. The architecture is simple enough for a microcontroller-based node: two analog multiplexers, a switchable reference and some arithmetic on the sampled record.

Two practical difficulties stand between this idea and a usable diagnosis, and they are the subject of this article. The first concerns the reference itself. The estimates of gain and offset are only as good as the knowledge of the levels that were applied. If the reference amplitude drifts and the nominal levels are used in the calculation, the drift is read as a fault of the electronics. The second difficulty concerns timing. The transient that follows a reference step carries the information about the AFE bandwidth, but the reference edge is generated by a sequencer that is in general not synchronized with the ADC clock. The edge therefore falls at an arbitrary position between two samples, and any descriptor that reads the response at fixed sample indices after the step changes with that position. A classifier trained with one edge placement then meets a different one in the field, or, as in this project, in a second simulator.

We address the first difficulty by measuring the applied reference levels with a monitor channel and by estimating the transfer parameters only from the settled part of each plateau. We address the second by replacing the fixed-sample descriptors with parameters of a first-order model fitted to the whole transient, in which the edge time is a free parameter that absorbs the unknown sampling phase. The resulting 20 descriptors are classified by a small multilayer perceptron (MLP) into the faulty layer (healthy, sensor, AFE or ADC) and, with a second network, into one of nine fault classes.

The study is a simulation study. The method is evaluated in a behavioral discrete-time model and in an ngspice model of the same idealized circuit, with component tolerances, noise, quantization, reference amplitudes within ±20% of nominal, monitor gain errors up to ±5% and five edge phases. The descriptor definitions, the compared variants, the test grids, the acceptance criteria and the decision rule were written into a protocol file before the validation data were generated, and the validation used new random seeds. No hardware was built.

The contributions of the article are the following.

1. An observability analysis of the two-point reference test, which states which parameters of the chain can be identified from the settled reference responses and shows how an error in the assumed reference levels maps onto apparent ADC and AFE parameters ({{S:arch}}).
2. Transient descriptors that are obtained from a separable least-squares fit with a free edge time and are, under stated assumptions, independent of the sampling phase and of the ADC gain and offset ({{S:proc}}).
3. An ablation that separates the effect of these descriptors from the effect of training with randomized edge phases, and a control experiment that quantifies what is lost when the reference levels are not monitored ({{S:results}}).
4. An evaluation under a frozen protocol in two simulation domains, with confidence intervals from a cluster bootstrap over operating conditions and with the weak classes reported alongside the strong ones ({{S:results}} and {{S:discussion}}).

The absolute classification performance is modest, and the article does not present the method as a complete fault detector. Its claim is narrower: within the simulated conditions, the localization of AFE and ADC faults is insensitive to the sampling phase and to the reference amplitude when the reference is monitored and the transient is described by a fit instead of by fixed samples.
