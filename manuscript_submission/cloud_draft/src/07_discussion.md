# Discussion <<sec:discussion>>

## What the Results Show <<sec:disc_findings>>

The experiments support three statements, each of which is tied to a mechanism that was identified before the data were generated.

The first concerns the reference. A two-point test localizes faults of the electronics only as long as the reference levels that enter the calibration are the levels that were applied. When they are not, the error does not spread evenly over the diagnosis; it takes the specific form predicted by {{EQ:mapping}}, an apparent change of the ADC gain. In the no-monitor control a reference that was 20% low or high reduced the layer macro-F1 from {{NOMON_K_x08}} and {{NOMON_K_x12}} to {{NOMON_U_x08}} and {{NOMON_U_x12}}, and most records were attributed to the ADC. With monitored levels, the classifier lost little even when the monitor itself had a gain error of up to ±5%. The practical reading is that the accuracy requirement of the test can be moved from the switched stimulus to a slow measurement of its levels, and that the requirement on this measurement is set by the smallest ADC gain fault that is to be distinguished, not by the drift of the reference.

The second concerns timing. Descriptors that read the step response at fixed sample indices lost {{ARM_bh_R1_RANGE}} in layer macro-F1 when the edge moved across one sampling interval. The loss was nearly the same in both simulators and was small at the phase used for training. Descriptors obtained from a fit with a free edge time removed the dependence to within {{G_PRR_MAX}}. The ablation adds a point that is of some general interest for learned diagnosis. Training with randomized phases, the data-driven remedy, recovered most of the pooled score but left a visible dependence on the phase, whereas the measurement-based remedy removed the dependence and made the randomization unnecessary. Where a nuisance variable has a known physical form, it can be cheaper and more reliable to estimate it and eliminate it from the features than to ask a classifier to become insensitive to it.

The third concerns the transfer between the two simulation domains, which was the problem that motivated the work. On the matched grid the loss was {{E_DROP}} with an interval that includes zero. Given that the two models share the same idealized circuit, this is a consistency result. It shows that the remaining differences between the domains (the numerical treatment of the edge, the switch resistances and the additional noise) no longer matter to the descriptors.

## Limits of the Diagnosis <<sec:disc_limits>>

Robustness to two nuisance variables is not the same as accurate diagnosis, and the results are explicit about the difference. The layer classifier assigns {{PCT:X_FLAG_bh}} of the healthy records to a fault layer in the behavioral domain. It therefore cannot serve as a fault detector, and a system that used its output directly would raise an alarm in most tests of a healthy interface. What it provides is conditional information: given that a fault of the electronics is present, the AFE and the ADC are told apart with a cross-confusion below {{C_MAX}}, and five of the nine fault classes are identified with a recall of at least {{X_MIN_STRONG}} in both domains.

The weak classes have a common cause. The two references excite the AFE and the ADC, and the test is active for these two stages only. For the sensor it remains a passive observation, with the limitation derived in {{S:observability}}: a bias or a sensitivity change of the sensor cannot be told from a change of the measured quantity without further knowledge. The simulated operating conditions vary the sensor baseline and amplitude over ranges that are comparable to the fault magnitudes, so the classifier has no basis for separating healthy records from records with these two faults, and no change of the classifier would create one. A remedy has to add information, for example a stimulus that acts on the sensing element itself, as in the self-test of micro-electromechanical sensors [@hantos2021; @deb2006], redundancy between sensors [@li2020], or a model of the measured process [@isermann2006]. The ADC clipping class is weak for a different reason, which is specific to the chosen reference levels: the fault is exercised only when the test signal reaches the clipping level. A reference level closer to the upper end of the converter range would be the obvious change, at the price of a test that itself approaches saturation. Neither remedy was evaluated here.

The dependence of the healthy-class assignments on the reference amplitude points to a further weakness. The classifiers were trained at nominal amplitude and without component tolerances, and the amplitudes of ±20% are an extrapolation. The secondary variant PRrM, whose training data contain tolerances, scored higher in both domains ({{S:res_ablation}}). Training with tolerances and with several amplitudes is therefore a plausible improvement, but the present experiment cannot separate it from the effect of adding a second simulation domain.

## Practical Implications <<sec:disc_practice>>

For a designer who considers such a test, the results suggest the following. The hardware consists of two analog multiplexers, a reference source with two levels for each injection point, and a monitor channel. The monitor must be independent of the converter under test, since a fault of a shared converter would cancel in the calibration ({{S:monitor}}); a second converter or a separate low-speed measurement path is needed. Its required accuracy is moderate. A gain error bound of ±5% was tolerated in the simulation, a figure that is tied to the smallest simulated ADC gain fault of 6% and would have to be tightened in proportion if smaller gain faults are to be recognized.

The sequencer that generates the reference edges does not need to be synchronized with the ADC clock, provided that the transient is described by the fitted model. The computation is a search over a fixed grid of 392 candidate pairs and 81 refinement points on 60 samples, followed by a network with about 7000 weights. The test interrupts the measurement: in the simulated sequence the sensor is disconnected for 240 ms, and the application must tolerate this gap or schedule the test in idle periods.

The descriptors are informative in their own right. The estimated gains, offsets and time constant are physical quantities with units, and a deviation in one of them indicates the stage concerned without a classifier ({{F:signatures}}). A deployment could combine thresholds on these quantities, set from the tolerances of the design, with the classifier, and could use repeated tests to suppress false alarms. This combination was not studied.

## Relation to Prior Work <<sec:disc_prior>>

The component-level self-test methods of Czaja [@czaja2013; @czaja2016] localize single faulty components inside an analog block, a finer resolution than the layer-level localization studied here, and one of them has been verified on hardware. The present work is complementary: it adds the separation of the converter from the analog block, which requires the second injection point, and it drops two assumptions, the known stimulus level and the synchronous sampling. The principle that an inaccurate stimulus can be tolerated if its error is identified is established in ADC linearity testing [@jin2005; @chen2018]; for the static gain of a cascade the error cannot be identified from the response alone ({{S:monitor}}), and a separate measurement takes its place.

Accuracies such as the 98.93% reported for the data-driven diagnosis of a single filter stage [@gao2024] should not be set against the scores of this study. The tasks differ, and so do the test conditions: the evaluation here includes component tolerances that were absent from training, reference amplitudes outside the training range, edge phases that were not controlled, a healthy class that competes with weakly observable sensor faults, and a split by operating condition. Under such conditions the absolute scores are low, and they are reported as they are.

## Limitations <<sec:limitations>>

The study has limitations that restrict the conclusions.

1. All results are simulation results. No circuit was built, and no measurement on a sensor, an analog front-end, a converter or a microcontroller was made. Execution time, memory and energy on an embedded target were not measured.
2. The two simulation domains implement the same idealized model: a first-order AFE, an affine converter with noise and quantization, ideal references and nearly ideal switches. Effects that a real circuit adds, such as amplifier nonlinearity and slew rate, a higher-order response, charge injection, reference settling and loading, leakage, temperature drift and converter nonlinearity, are absent. The first-order model that the descriptors assume is the model that generated the data. The fit-quality descriptor is intended to reveal a violation of this assumption, but its behavior for responses that are not first-order was not tested.
3. The monitor channel is a model with a common gain error, noise and quantization. Its offset is assumed to be trimmed, and gain errors that differ between the four levels were not simulated.
4. The edge phase is constant within a record and identical for all edges, and five phases were tested. Jitter of the edges and a drift of the phase during a test were not simulated.
5. The plateau means are not fully settled for slow AFEs ({{S:plateau}}), so the static estimates are biased in a way that depends on the time constant. The classifier is given settling-error descriptors, but the estimates themselves are not corrected.
6. The faults are single, parametric and constant during a record. Multiple, intermittent and catastrophic faults were not simulated, and the severity ranges are assumptions of the simulation.
7. The classifiers were trained at nominal amplitude without tolerances. Three data sets and two initializations per model were used; the confidence intervals reflect the finite number of test conditions and not the variability between data sets or training runs.
8. The simulator and the training code were not available when this article was prepared ({{S:repro}}). The results were regenerated from saved predictions, and the simulations themselves could not be repeated.
